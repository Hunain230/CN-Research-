Abstract
A SOC analyst has to decide which alerts describe the same incident before deciding what to inspect
first. That decision can be distorted by copied observations, late-arriving evidence, and claims that
disagree about an endpoint. We specify bounded correlation (C), prioritization (P), and verification (V)
agent roles and test their software contracts with an offline adapter and a small local Qwen prototype.
The measured experiment is separate: a frozen, deterministic C/V/P pipeline with a provenance gate.
On a fresh synthetic holdout of 15 queues and 5,018 eligible alerts, its endpoint fallback raised incident
Recall@20 from 250/300 (83.33%) to 260/300 (86.67%), recoverability from 263/300 (87.67%) to
281/300 (93.67%), and P@10 from 107/150 (71.33%) to 113/150 (75.33%). Neither arm formed
a false grouped pair under simulator truth. After prediction files were sealed, an audit found 711
accepted genuine relationship tokens, no accepted false tokens, 35 genuine rejections, and 150 false
rejections. The candidate had missed its development gate by one incident; testing it unchanged on the
fresh holdout followed a documented protocol adjustment. A separate retrospective GUIDE reference
reached 49.82% Recall@20 on 499 queues. The local LLM run does not establish a model advantage,
and the available GUIDE export lacks the original roots and evidence-arrival times required to test
the frozen verifier on operational data.
Keywords: security operations center, alert correlation, large language models, evidence provenance,
multi-agent systems, incident prioritization
1 Introduction
An analyst opening a SOC queue sees detector
alerts, not neatly bounded incidents. Several alerts
may describe one attack, but deciding which ones
belong together is harder than finding a shared
host or account. A second log entry, for example,
may be a copy of the first; its event time may be
early even though the entry reached the SOC after
the decision was made. Treating either as indepen-
dent, timely confirmation can merge two unrelated
investigations. Existing work has studied aggrega-
tion, attack reconstruction, and triage from several
angles [1–4]. Our starting point is the evidence
behind an individual merge.
We therefore ask what can be joined using
only evidence available at a fixed decision time.
Correlation builds initial fragments and proposes
further links. Verification checks whether each
cross-fragment proposal has the required original
observations and consistent fields. Only then does
prioritization order the resulting groups for review.
1

A better rank cannot recover an incident whose
alerts were never grouped. This distinction mat-
ters because alert reduction, path reconstruction,
severity prediction, and queue retrieval use differ-
ent targets [3, 5, 6]. Figure 1 shows the actual
execution order.
Three questions guide the study. First, what
does the verifier accept or refuse when claims
are copied, late, missing, or inconsistent? Second,
on the same untouched synthetic queues, does a
narrowly defined endpoint fallback recover more
incidents without an observed increase in incorrect
groupings? Third, which missing data prevent the
same test on a real SOC export? We contribute:
• bounded contracts for the three agent roles,
alongside the rules actually executed by the
frozen deterministic verifier;
• a paired fresh holdout, reported at the inci-
dent, queue, group, and relationship-token levels,
including queues where retrieval worsened;
• an account of what the local LLM and GUIDE
investigations did establish, and of the fields still
needed for an external test.
The measured holdout does not compare our sys-
tem with a published system on shared data. We
keep that boundary explicit throughout the paper.
2 Related work
2.1 Aggregation, reconstruction, and
prioritization
Landauer et al. learn aggregation patterns from
semi-structured
alerts
[1];
Wang
et
al.
use
similarity-based aggregation before Transformer-
based attack prediction [2]; and GRAIN recon-
structs multi-stage scenarios from a learned causal
graph [3]. These studies motivate useful ways to
organize a queue. Our experiment asks a narrower
question about the evidence for a proposed edge:
were the supporting observations original, indepen-
dently rooted, and visible when the decision was
taken? The studies use different inputs and eval-
uation targets, so their published scores are not
direct comparators.
Other work starts from the triage decision itself:
Sadlek et al. use kill-chain graphs to order inci-
dents by severity [4], while CyberShapley identifies
informative parts of an anomaly graph [5]. In our
evaluation, an incident must first have a sufficiently
complete and pure group, and that group must
then reach the first 20 queue positions. Provenance
is also well established. HOLMES, POIROT, UNI-
CORN, and CrossHound use information flows,
audit trails, or host/network relationships for inves-
tigation [7–10]. We build on that line of work by
specifying a three-root decision gate with evidence-
arrival time for this controlled grouping task; we
do not claim to introduce provenance reasoning.
2.2 Evidence assumptions and
evaluation
Reviews of security-event and log correlation docu-
ment differences in data sources, missingness, and
metrics [6, 11]. These differences are material here.
A source ID does not prove that two real sensors are
independent; the controlled generator supplies root
identities, and the verifier rejects copies that trace
back to the same root. The cited studies address
aggregation, prediction, reconstruction, triage, or
investigation with distinct labels and inputs; their
published scores are not a same-data comparison
with this experiment.
3 Proposed methodology
For queue q, let Aq(T) be alerts whose ledger-
recorded evidence availability minute is at most
cutoff T = 100. Each alert has an identifier, source
identifier, observation identifier, event minute,
host, account, process context, severity, and asset
criticality. A relationship token r = (a, b) is accom-
panied by claims from process, authentication, and
network-flow families. A claim has an observa-
tion identifier, upstream-root identifier, optional
derived from pointer, event-field assertions, and
availability minute. Original means the derivation
pointer is null; a derived copy remains dependent
on its origin.
The algorithm outputs a partition Gq
=
(G1, . . . , Gm) of Aq(T), ordered by a determin-
istic group score. Initial grouping uses source,
process-tree, and selected lineage context; the extra
verification stage is applied to cross-fragment claim
tokens. An edge proposal is evidence for a possi-
ble union, whereas a group is a transitive partition
component. The simulator’s expert priority list and
incident assignments are restricted to the evaluator.
They are never model-visible fields.

3.1 Agent framework and executable
contracts
3.1.1 Roles and evidence boundary
A request contains opaque alert IDs, one approved
evidence snapshot ET , and explicit candidate,
token, schema, prompt, and model-version budgets.
The receiver rejects evaluator labels or lineage,
cross-organization records, invalid citation IDs,
duplicate keys, non-finite scores, excessive evidence,
and unexpected JSON fields. Observation text is
treated as data rather than an instruction. All roles
receive the same cutoff-time snapshot.
For a supplied pair (a, b), correlation (C)
returns LINK, NO LINK, or UNSURE, with uncer-
tainty, reason, and supporting/conflicting evidence
IDs. It cannot search for new pairs. A bounded
builder joins validated LINK edges; prioritization
(P) scores the resulting candidate against other
groups and cites its rationale. Verification (V)
marks substantive claims supported, unsupported,
unverifiable, or contradicted and returns PASS,
REVISE, or REVIEW. At most one revision is
allowed. Unresolved conflict, unsupported priority,
invalid output, and provider failure route to review.
The score is a ranking signal, not a calibrated
attack probability.

3.1.2 Execution and measured scope
The orchestrator validates pairs in stable order,
records decisions and abstentions, permits one
revision, and keys replay to input, role, schema,
prompt, and model versions. Offline-adapter tests
exercised four synthetic pairs, ten role calls, one
revision, and one review; 34 assertions checked
roles, citations, leakage, malformed output, caps,
and replay, while 59 more checked evaluation and
ranking boundaries. A bounded local qwen2.5:3b
Ollama prototype ran on 20 organizations, but its
pair universe differed from the statistical reference.
Rules and BM25 have synthetic interfaces; single-
call and repeated-call LLM controls use offline
stubs. None of these contract tests establishes

3.2 Architecture and data flow
Fig. 1 shows the computational order. The cor-
relation routine first groups same-source alerts,
process-tree references, and eligible lineage refer-
ences with host/account guards and a component-
size cap of 100. It returns a partition and candidate
diagnostics. Verification then inspects each visible
relationship-token bundle before any additional
cross-fragment union. Finally the ranking routine
orders the resulting groups for the analyst. The
repository’s earlier code labels this as C–P–V in
places, but the frozen prediction call executes corre-
lation, then verification, then scoring; we describe
this actual execution order as C/V/P.
Verification outcomes in the implementation
are reason-coded. A mismatched event or causal
field, late claim, shared root, derived-only family,
excessive component size, or incompatible com-
ponent host/account context prevents union. An
absent process claim may be considered by the con-
strained fallback in Section 3.3; its extra check uses
receiver-visible endpoint fields, not truth labels.
Rejected and insufficient proposals leave the exist-
ing partition intact. This is a conservative decision
procedure under its assumed root semantics, not a
formal guarantee against adversarially coordinated
false observations.
3.3 Evidence verification and
endpoint fallback
3.3.1 Visibility and initial fragments
Before correlation, the receiver checks that the
evidence ledger is contiguous, has one availability
entry per alert, agrees with alert-level availability,
and contains no private fields. At cutoff T = 100,
it keeps only arrived alerts and claims whose two
endpoints are eligible. The initial pass groups same-
source and process-tree fragments, then considers
available lineage references. Host, account, and size
guards apply to the components at that stage. The
later claim verifier does not go back and certify
every pair made within a source. This matters
when interpreting the zero false-pair result: it was
observed in the holdout, not guaranteed by the
verifier’s logic.
3.3.2 Original independent attestation
For each proposed relationship, the baseline
inspects a bundle of claims. To join two fragments
it needs one original process claim, one original
authentication claim, and one original network
claim, all from different upstream roots and all
available by T. Endpoint and event identities, host,
account, process parent, session, and flow fields
must agree. The event-time check is tb ≥ta−3 and
|tb −ta| ≤25 minutes. A copy of an original obser-
vation does not supply a missing family. Finally,
the two current components must overlap on host
and account and contain at most 100 alerts after
union.

3.3.3 Constrained endpoint fallback
The candidate revisits just one failure reason:
missing family. If the process claim is absent,
it may use original endpoint observations along-
side one original authentication and one original
network-flow claim. The two claims must have dif-
ferent roots and be available by minute 100. The
endpoints must be original, have different source
and observation IDs, and share an endpoint-origin
root distinct from both claim roots. Host, account,
event timing, process parent, session, and flow must
agree between endpoints and claims. The existing
component guards still apply. Thus the endpoint
context fills the missing process-family role; it can-
not excuse a contradiction, a late claim, or another
failure. Figure 2 shows the branches.

3.3.4 Decision semantics and
component state
Claim validity and permission to merge are sep-
arate checks. A valid token may point to alerts
already in one component; then there is noth-
ing to join. It may also pass its claim checks
but fail because the proposed combined group
would exceed 100 alerts or the components have no
host/account overlap. Existing groups stay intact
in either case. Bundles are processed in sorted
order, with union–find tracking the additional
verified joins. The three-root requirement counts
the original observations for a token, not alerts
accumulated in its component.
An authentication copy retains its upstream
root; a late claim is unavailable even when its event
time is early. Contradictory causal fields block a
union. Only an absent process-family witness can
trigger the guarded endpoint substitution.

4 Experimental setup
We generated queues with process, authentica-
tion, and network-flow claims. The generator also
recorded whether an observation was original or
derived, when it became available, and whether
fields were missing or contradictory. Eight training
queues and ten development queues informed the
method. On the latter, the candidate recalled 169
of 200 expert top-20 incidents, recovered 177, and
credited 73 of 100 P@10 positions; it had no false
grouped pairs or mixed groups. The planned gate
was 170/200 Recall@20, so the candidate missed it
by one incident. The team documented a decision
5

to test the candidate unchanged on a new hold-
out. We report this as a protocol adjustment, not
a gate pass.
The fresh sample used seed 31103 and tem-
plate variant 2: 15 queues, 5,018 cutoff-eligible
alerts, and 300 expert top-20 incident opportu-
nities. Both methods received identical inputs.
Source/configuration hashes were fixed and predic-
tion files SHA-256 sealed before private truth was
opened. Each arm ran once; the holdout remains
within the generator family. Its audit recorded
345 false independent-looking claims, 170 derived
copies, 95 false same-context tokens, 55 late claims,
and 28 missing true claims. These overlapping
challenge categories are not a partition.

reproducibility
The controlled evidence generator is the dataset

5.4 Development-only and historical

Separate development studies examined ranking

6 Comparison with base papers
and state of the art
We chose Hmimou et al. [12] as the primary base
paper in the Phase 1 review because it puts special-
ized LLM agents to work on threat correlation. Its
email, log, and IP agents combine LLaMA 3.3-70B
with retrieval and security tools. On the authors’
evaluation, the reported figures are 93.6% sys-
tem accuracy, 87.0% correlation precision, 41.3%
fewer false positives, and 38.5% less analyst triage
time than their SIEM control. Their question is
broader than ours, but their study does not test
whether a sufficiently complete expert-priority inci-
dent reaches the top 20 of a ranked queue. The
four figures belong to their experiment and are not
our results.
Guo et al. [13] helps frame the other half of
our topic, alert priority. Their system combines an
FFT–PCC risk gate, BERT-assisted evidence han-
dling, graph context, and AHP ranking; they report
F1 of 0.910 for false-positive identification and a
separate 45.2% response-time reduction. Its online
components are function-specific rather than an
autoregressive generative LLM pipeline. AgentSOC
by Roy and Singh [14] instead generates hypotheses
and ranks response actions after structural simu-
lation. Neither paper reports the expert-labeled
top-20 incident-queue outcome used here.
Our extension makes the evidence gate explicit
at receiver time and evaluates both grouping and
queue retrieval. Against our own matched synthetic
baseline, endpoint fallback adds 3.33 percentage

7 Ablation study
The ten development queues hold the cohort
fixed while varying the evidence rule (Fig. 7).
Rich claims connected more fragments but pro-
duced 2,580 false within-group pairs and
22 mixed groups. Independent-root verification
removed those observed errors and yielded 158/200
Recall@20 with a fixed rank bonus. Endpoint
fallback then raised Recall@20 to 169/200 and
recoverability from 166/200 to 177/200 on the
same queues; its 14 new unions were genuine under
generator truth. It missed the planned 170/200
development gate by one. No development count
is added to the fresh holdout. Other investigations
used separate development cohorts. One hard-
negative ranker left groups unchanged but moved
matched R@20 from 263/300 to 262/300. Delay-
ing or removing independent evidence also reduced
reconstruction. We do not pool those trials with
the primary holdout or describe them as holdout
ablations. There is still no eligible real-queue com-
parison of C/P/V against a single LLM, so the
contribution of LLM agent count has not been
measured.
8 Discussion
The guarded endpoint substitution added 18 recov-
erable incidents but ten top-20 recalls. The eight-
incident difference shows that reconstruction and
ranking are separate bottlenecks. Zero observed
false pairs does not establish independence of real
upstream sources.
Copies retain their root and late evidence is
invisible regardless of event time. Delayed, miss-
ing, or contradictory observations reduced recovery
in development. A pairwise-trained ranker moved
matched development Recall@20 from 263/300
to 262/300 with unchanged groups, illustrating
objective mismatch.
The strict rule rejected or abstained on 35 gen-
uine proposed tokens. Analyst preference for this
tradeoff depends on telemetry and missed-incident
cost, neither measured here.
8.1 Threats to validity
The generator supplies truth, roots, and causal
fields. Three simulated roots may correspond to
dependent or compromised real feeds; the fresh
template variant remains within the development
generator family. Fifteen queues cannot bound an
operational error rate.
Testing despite a one-incident development-
gate miss was a recorded protocol adjustment
before holdout generation. Results depend on the
80% completeness/purity rule and simulated pri-
ority labels. Large groups dominate pair counts,
and initial within-source groups bypass the later
three-root verifier. Distinct diagnostic cohorts are
not paired with this holdout.
Real false unions, analyst benefit, workload,
latency distribution, and abstention cost remain
unmeasured.
8.2 External evaluation readiness
The local Microsoft GUIDE export contains
4,147,992 evidence rows, 637,320 composite alert
keys, and 9,980 ranking rows. Of the alert
keys, 70,702 have conflicting incident assignments.
Timestamp perturbation [15] and the absence of
evidence-arrival times, original roots, derivation
chains, and causal claim bundles prevent an equiv-
alent point-in-time verifier evaluation. Queue/rank
semantics and approved model-visible fields also
remain unresolved.
No GUIDE prediction or real-data performance
result is attributed to the frozen verifier.
9 Conclusion and future work

could sometimes stand in for a missing process

synthetic queues, that rule raised Recall@20
from 250/300 to 260/300 and recoverability
from 263/300 to 281/300; neither arm pro-
duced a false grouped pair or mixed group under
simulator truth. The candidate missed its devel-
