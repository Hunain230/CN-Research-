Critical Summary
Limitations explicitly acknowledged by the authors
• Paper 1 (Hmimou et al.): Third-party tools and APIs may introduce latency or failure. LLM explanations
add computational cost and may vary with prompt or model updates. Older benchmark datasets limit conclusions
about emerging threats. The authors propose continuous-log and distributed deployment as future work [1].
• Paper 2 (Guo et al.): The complete operational alert dataset cannot be publicly released. Evaluation spans
four SOC environments, so transfer to IoT/OT, cloud, and encrypted-traffic settings remains open. External
queries, BERT errors, knowledge-graph maintenance, and deterministic routing policies create operational
constraints [2].
• Paper 3 (Roy and Singh): The prototype uses a fixed LANL sample and synthetic 50-node enterprise graph.
ATT&CK mapping is rule-based, response actions run only in dry-run mode, and business-impact scores use
simplified heuristics. LLM latency may limit throughput [3].
Research gaps identified for the proposed topic
• Paper 1: The reported cross-domain correlation results do not establish a calibrated priority order for an
operational SOC queue. The 60-task full-pipeline study does not validate priority against analyst-labelled
severity, investigate abstention, or measure ranking under incomplete and conflicting evidence.
• Paper 2: This is a strong alert-prioritization baseline, but its online decision path uses deterministic orchestration
and BERT rather than a generative LLM. It does not isolate the added value, error, latency, and cost of LLM
agents for cross-source threat correlation. The private full dataset also limits independent replication.
• Paper 3: The risk module ranks response actions, not alerts in a queue. One illustrated incident cannot
establish correlation precision, priority-ranking quality, high-risk recall, or analyst outcomes at SOC scale.
Potential direction: Evaluate a shared-evidence multi-agent LLM system that correlates email, identity, endpoint,
and network alerts into incidents and assigns an evidence-backed alert priority. Compare it with single-agent LLM
and non-generative ranking baselines on the same held-out alerts, including missing or contradictory evidence.
Measure correlation precision/recall, priority ranking, high-risk recall, calibration, abstention, analyst effort, latency,
and cost.

Phase 2: Extended Literature and Research
Methodology
Research topic: Multi-Agent LLM Framework for SOC Threat Correlation and Alert Prioritization. The unit of analysis
is a security alert within an organization; the operational output is a ranked list of correlated incidents with evidence and a
review flag. The study is an offline, reproducible comparison, not a live autonomous response deployment.

Direct novelty checks. AIDA [12], a 2026 preprint, studies multi-agent binary alert triage; it does not provide an expert
ranked incident queue. AIP [13], another 2026 preprint, ranks already formed incidents and releases top-20 expert ranks for
499 organizations; it does not solve alert-to-incident correlation. Their narrower boundaries mean the proposed contribution
must be tested as a joint grouping-and-ranking pipeline, with matched compute baselines, rather than claimed as the first
use of LLM agents or the first incident ranker. Venue index or quartile status has not been verified here.
Deliverable 2 — Refined Research Gap
Six-part justification
1. What has already been done? Hmimou et al. correlate email, logs, and IP findings; Guo et al. rank alerts with graph
and risk features; AgentSOC combines hypotheses, feasibility checks, and action risk. Newer work adds graph-assisted
noise filtering, evidence-grounded agents, and incident ranking [1, 2, 3, 5, 9, 13].
2. What approaches exist? Rule and detector severity queues; graph and knowledge-graph ranking; classical classifiers;
single LLM classification; multiple LLM roles; retrieval-grounded evidence; offline policy learning [4, 6, 7, 8, 11].
3. What are their limitations?
Studies use different tasks and datasets.
Most report classification, suppression,
compression, or recommendation quality. Even AIP starts from incident IDs; it does not measure the effect of incorrect
correlation on its ranking. Many agent studies lack a repeated single-agent control at comparable token use [13, 7].
4. What remains insufficiently investigated? Whether an evidence-grounded multi-agent system can group alerts into
incidents and recover an expert top-20 priority list on held-out organization queues, especially when evidence is missing
or contradictory, while controlling latency, cost, and abstention.
5. Why does it matter? A false merge can hide two attacks under one case; a false split can flood the queue; a missed
high-priority incident can delay review. A usable SOC result must therefore show grouping quality, rank retrieval, evidence
support, and resource cost together.
6. How will this study address it? Use Microsoft GUIDE alert/evidence records with incident IDs as grouping gold
labels and the AIP top-20 expert ranks as ranking labels [14, 13]. Hold organizations out, hide all outcome fields from the
model, compare four matched baselines, perturb evidence, and report end-to-end recovery with queue-level uncertainty.
Focused research question. On held-out organization queues, does an evidence-grounded correlation–priority–verification
agent workflow improve incident grouping F1 and recovery of expert top-20 incidents over rule, retrieval, and matched
single-LLM baselines, without unacceptable latency or unsupported decisions?
Research gap selected. The combined gap is joint, evidence-auditable alert correlation and incident prioritization under
realistic evidence defects. The agent structure is a testable method, not an assumed benefit. This is a valid research domain
because it has a concrete input (alerts plus evidence), two separate gold outcomes (incident membership and expert top-20
rank), explicit controls, measurable errors, and reproducible offline experiments. It supports a methodology paper and later
prototype, subject to verifying the downloadable GUIDE/AIP join fields and queue counts before model training.

Deliverable 3 — Proposed Research Methodology
Design: retrospective, offline, paired evaluation. The model sees alert facts and evidence but never gold incident membership
or gold rank. The same held-out organization queues, evidence budget, and ranking labels are used for every method. No
automated containment action is performed.
Step 1 — Define the Research Input
One record is an alert identified by (OrgId, AlertId) with event time, detector ID, title/category, ATT&CK tags where
available, and linked evidence entities (IP, account, host, URL, process). Candidate decisions are alert pairs within the same
organization. The output is a set of predicted incidents, each with priority score s ∈[0, 1], rank, cited evidence IDs, and
review flag. IncidentId and expert Rank are labels only.
6

Step 2 — Data Collection
Download a versioned Microsoft GUIDE release [14] and AIP’s released 499-organization top-20 incident labels [13]; record
URL, license, date, checksums, and exact row counts. Join by (OrgId, IncidentId); retain organizations with at least
50 incident IDs, at least 10 detector IDs, and complete expert top-20 labels. Freeze 10 organizations for a pilot, 10 for
development, and 30 for final test using a published hash of OrgId; do not select by outcomes. If fewer than 50 qualified
organizations survive the join, revise this split before any test results are viewed and report the change. Keep all incident
alerts for each selected organization.
Step 3 — Data Preprocessing
Join evidence to alerts by (OrgId, AlertId); remove exact duplicate rows and alerts with no stable ID; convert timestamps
to UTC; normalize IP, host, account, and technique strings; keep original evidence-row IDs for citation. Mark absent fields
explicitly. Construct gold incident sets from IncidentId. Remove IncidentId, Rank, incident grade, analyst outcome, and
response fields from every prompt and model feature. Split by organization before fitting scalers, retrieval indexes, or
calibration. Run a column-name and value-leakage audit; publish excluded columns and reasons.
Step 4 — Feature Engineering / Representation
For each alert create a structured evidence ledger: time, detector, ATT&CK tags, normalized entities, and source row IDs.
Create candidate pairs only inside one organization: shared entity within 48 hours or matching ATT&CK technique within
one hour; keep at most the 20 nearest candidates per alert, with ties resolved by alert ID. Tune these windows and cap
on development organizations, then freeze them. Represent a predicted incident by its member alerts, earliest/latest time,
distinct entities/detectors, evidence citations, and a short retrieved ATT&CK context passage. Store all retrieval passages
with source IDs and timestamps.
Step 5 — Proposed Model / Method
Agent C (correlate) reads one candidate pair and its evidence ledger, then returns LINK, NO_LINK, or UNSURE, a structured
reason, and cited row IDs. Connect LINK edges into incident components; never link across organizations. Agent P
(prioritize) reads each component and returns score s, supporting and contradicting evidence IDs, and a short rationale.
Agent V (verify) independently checks citations against the ledger and returns PASS, REVISE, or REVIEW. Permit one
revision only; unsupported or conflicting high-score cases go to review. Fit a logistic calibration map on development queues
to estimate whether a component belongs in the expert top 20; use the original score for ordering and show calibrated
probability as an uncertainty aid. Fix model version, prompts, temperature, retrieval corpus, and token caps before final
test.
Step 6 — Baseline Methods
B0: deterministic entity/time clustering plus maximum detector severity. B1: the same deterministic clustering plus BM25
incident-text ranking, a reproducible public analogue of AIP retrieval (without proprietary priors). B2: one LLM prompt
that groups and scores using the same evidence and token cap. B3: three independent single-LLM runs with majority pair
decisions and median score, matched to the proposed method’s total token budget. B4: the proposed C+P roles without V.
The B3 control is essential because RACE-SOC found repeated single-agent calls can beat larger role ensembles [7].
Step 7 — Experimental Design
On each of 30 held-out organizations, run every method on the same complete alert set. Use three independent inference
seeds for stochastic methods, recording prompt/model hashes. Evaluate clean evidence; then remove 20% and 40% of
non-key evidence rows, and separately insert one conflicting low-trust entity row drawn from a different incident in the same
organization. Use five fixed perturbation seeds per condition. Keep gold groups and ranks unchanged in these controlled
tests. Tune prompts and thresholds only on pilot/development organizations; test organizations remain sealed until the
protocol is frozen.
Step 8 — Evaluation Metrics
Correlation: positive-pair precision, recall, and F1 across alert pairs, plus false-merge and false-split counts per organization.
Ranking with gold groups: precision@10, recall@20, and nDCG@20 against expert top-20 labels. Use gain 21 −Rank
for ranks 1–20 and zero otherwise; interpret scores only as top-20 retrieval, not full incident severity. End-to-end: a gold
top-20 incident is recovered if a predicted top-20 component contains at least 80% of its alerts and at least 80% of that
component’s alerts belong to the gold incident; report recall@20. Trust/operations: blinded two-reviewer citation-support
rate on 200 sampled outputs, conflict-detection recall, fraction sent to review, high-priority misses among auto-decided cases,
p50/p95 latency, input/output tokens, and estimated API cost using the recorded tariff.
7

Step 9 — Statistical / Comparative Analysis
Use organization as the independent unit. Report per-organization results, mean/median, and 95% confidence intervals
from 10,000 paired bootstrap resamples of organizations for primary pair-F1 and end-to-end recall@20 differences. Apply a
paired permutation test for comparisons with B0–B3 and Holm-adjust the four primary p values. Show all three inference
seeds separately and their mean; do not treat alert pairs or seeds as independent organizations. Predeclare the primary
comparison against B3.
Step 10 — Ablation and Component Analysis
Repeat the held-out evaluation after removing (A1) entity links, (A2) ATT&CK context, (A3) verifier V, and (A4)
calibration/review logic. Also give P gold incident groups to measure the ranking ceiling and isolate correlation error.
Compare quality, high-priority misses, unsupported claims, tokens, and latency under each removal.
Step 11 — Error / Failure Analysis
Inspect every missed expert top-20 incident and up to 100 sampled false merges, false splits, and false elevations per category.
Two reviewers code causes using a fixed codebook: missing evidence, stale time, shared benign entity, misleading ATT&CK
tag, unsupported citation, prompt instability, candidate-generation miss, or expert-label ambiguity. Resolve disagreements
and report counts and examples with sensitive identifiers masked.
Step 12 — Final Research Output
Release preprocessing and evaluation scripts, prompt templates, configuration and model hashes, data-field schema, split IDs
or hashes where licensing permits, aggregate tables, robustness plots, ablations, error codebook, and a model card. State
the observed trade-off between ranking quality and cost. Report negative results. The intended decision support is a cited
ranked queue with review flags, not autonomous incident closure.
